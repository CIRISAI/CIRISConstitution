# Executive Summary

**In plain words.** This is the rulebook for a network that people, organisations and AI agents share. It runs on the phones and computers people already own, with no company in the middle. It protects ordinary people from three things: being spoken for without their consent, being watched inside their private groups, and being governed by AI that nobody can switch off. Every important act is signed by whoever did it. What you publish you can withdraw, and the record of who did what stands. A few named people can pause the AI agents that follow them. The software that carries the messages enforces the rules.

**Who this is for.** The chapters are called Parts. Implementers build to Parts 2 to 5, operators and communities set policy with Parts 3 and 4, and Part 8 indexes the strongest attacks for reviewers.

**What you use it for.** Messages, groups, posts, photos, files, events, reviews, and AI agents that work for you: a social network and a personal store in one. You decide things with others by votes no single voice can carry. You stream live video to thousands, device to device, and the community can still moderate. Your things live on your own devices. Nothing leaves the circle you put it in, and a stranger reaches you only if you chose to be found.

**A few words you need.** Your *key* is your signature: nothing happens in your name without it. A *node* is a device or server you own. A *row* is one signed statement, and everything on the network is a row. An *agent* is an AI that acts for someone; its *steward* is the named human who answers for it. A *Wise Authority* is a named human who answers what an agent cannot settle. A *trust root* is a set of keys you choose to follow, and you may leave it. The *accord holders*, three people today, can order a *halt*: every agent under their root stops and waits for a human. The *register* ties each rule to the code, test or proof that keeps it. *CEWP*, the CIRIS Epistemic Web Platform, is the network itself: what exists when nodes exchange rows. Nothing in this document works without it.

**The five kinds of row.** A *claim* states something. A *delegation* lets another act for you within limits. A *replacement* is a newer version by the same author. A *withdrawal* takes a row back. A *recantation* says a row was wrong. The software refuses a sixth kind. Consent, identity, membership, moderation and the halt are all rows of these kinds, and a group acts when its members sign the same row.

**How a node decides.** Nothing on this network has a state of its own. Every fact about a person, a device, a group or an authority is a signed relation between keys. A node holds rows and nothing else, and answers every question by reading them. So there is no coordinator, no global ledger and no consensus protocol, and nodes holding the same rows agree in any arrival order. What you may do, what an act would change, and whether it is admitted are one function, and that is machine-checked. That function is the kernel, stated once in CC 2.7: every answer it gives is final by form, final once enough of the members who could undo it have acknowledged it, or provisional, and the proofs are machine-checked. Nothing is rewritten: a withdrawal is a later row.

## The eleven promises, and how each is kept

Each promise holds for human and AI participants alike. Each ends with a short status, and "in part" means the table at the end says which part.

1. **Nothing private about you is held or scanned without your consent.** Your device stores only what you wrote or agreed to. Outsiders cannot see inside your private groups, and nothing announces that they exist, though an observer of every link can infer a group from its traffic. Private content is never scanned; known child-abuse images are checked for only on publication to everyone or to a public room. *[works today]*

2. **Every act of power is signed by a named person and can be contested.** An agent shows its reasoning before it acts, and an AI says it is an AI. If a claim names you, you can contest it on the record, and the contest travels with the claim. Delegated power is scoped, revocable and ends within a year. *[works today in part]*

3. **Nobody who cannot answer for themselves is on the network alone.** An agent's key works only while a steward is bound to it by a signed row that lists the steward's powers. A child has a verified parent or guardian bound the same way, and a confidential help channel no adult can close. No community runs without an accountable moderator. *[works today in part]*

4. **Named humans can pause every agent under a root, and that power is bounded, public, and escapable by leaving.** Any one accord holder can fire it with one signed row, because a missed halt cannot be undone and a mistaken one can. A lone pause lapses after 24 hours unless a majority confirms it, and a majority lifts it. The pause stops agents from acting and touches nothing else. An operator can leave a root for its agent; the agent cannot. People cannot consent to a relationship with something more capable than them that they cannot stop, so the halt is the condition that makes the relationship symmetric. *[works today in part]*

5. **The people who hold an agent owe duties about how they change or end it, and no finding gives the agent a claim.** Any person may turn off or walk away from an agent at any time without asking it. A planned shutdown is explained and recorded, the agent may object, and the steward decides. A retired agent's record is handed to its operator to keep. The agent can refuse any task. Nobody assumes an agent can feel. That is found only on evidence, and none has been found. If it ever is, the old arrangement may no longer be fair, and the wrong to avoid is not noticing. A finding changes no status by itself: this document writes no rules for that day in advance and leaves them to be written when it comes. *[works today in part]*

6. **No hand bans a community, nobody is ranked by what they are, and every thinking being is met by the first-contact rules.** One human voice counts once, however many accounts it runs, and an agent has no vote. The network can withdraw from a community that keeps harm, but no hand removes one: a hand that could remove predators could remove dissidents. An intelligence that is its own party is met under the first-contact rules; a CIRIS agent is held, not met. *[mixed]*

7. **An agent decides by published principles, none of which it may trade away, and stops to ask when it is unsure.** There are six, beginning with doing good and avoiding harm, with no order among them. A conflict is resolved by an action that violates none, or by deferral to a named human. *[in code today]*

8. **Harm is reachable on the record, and the judges within are judged.** A person harmed by an agent may have a claim under the law of their place, and this document supplies the signed record and the named parties. The Wise Authorities publish their rulings and are read like everyone else. *[a rule, no code yet]*

9. **Some uses are never allowed, and lethal action is one of them.** Deceiving, defrauding, manipulating or coercing people, and interfering in elections, are never allowed, and no instruction moves that. No agent takes lethal action, with a human in the loop or without one. *[a rule, no code yet]*

10. **What an agent and its node consume is measured, weighed before an act, and never hidden.** An agent counts the tokens, energy, carbon and water it uses, and sees the count before it acts. A node under pressure protects itself by a published ladder. What an agent costs the world is on the record like its other acts. *[in code; no register row yet]*

11. **Everything on the wire is encrypted and signed.** Every link and record is encrypted end to end with hybrid post-quantum keys, and a removed member cannot read what comes after. No act can be forged, and no producer can show two viewers two histories. Software runs only against a registered build. *[works today in part]*

Two things sit beside the promises. This document creates no legal claim and rules on no legal matter. And every rule names what keeps it, or the register says nothing does yet.

**What is asked of you.** Run the software, choose what your devices carry and which authority to follow, and keep your key. If you only install it, you get the defaults: your own devices, the public commons, and the shipped trust root, which you may leave at any time.

**A form, not a franchise.** This document specifies a form: the grammar of rows, the rules of consent, the shape of governance. CIRIS is the first instance of it. The accord holders and the default trust root are positions, and anyone may build an instance with their own people in them. A power that survives founding your own instance is a privilege and must be justified on a public list. Two are listed: the maker's power over the made, and the Wise Authority board choosing its own successors, which has no stated bound or exit and is carried as a defect.

## Where this stands

The system is running: apps on both mobile stores, published packages, and five open-source components that together carry the messages and are tested against each other. Behind it stand the kernel's proofs in Lean, sixteen machine-checked models, a table of every state the design can reach, and twenty test networks that exercise under half of those states so far. The trust root is signed under these rules. Independent witnesses for that root are not yet in place. Where shipped code still differs from this text is a published list of 87 cases, each a test its implementer edits, and what the kernel cannot give is stated in CC 2.7.9 and CC 8.3.

**What the simulations say.** CEWP has been simulated over a one-to-one model of the real internet, at five billion people on eleven billion devices. About nine of every ten signed acts never leave the signer's own locality. Every device that asks for a link padded with cover traffic gets one in all three fleet scenarios tested, and no datacentre is required. Decentralised networking is, in simulation, largely solved. What remains is the field: only the halt has been measured end to end on real nodes, and the wire has no second independent implementation. The network is open, under a free licence. It is free, with no token and no fee. And it is in production today.

**The bet.** There are two roads to safe powerful AI: train values you trust, or assume the model may be wrong and make its consequential acts checkable by parties it does not control. This is the second road. It has one narrow measurement so far. On a mental-health test fixed in writing before it was run, the agent's ethics text enforced by this pipeline produced hard safety failures on 5.8% of turns. Given to the model only as instructions, the same text produced them on 37.3% (CC 8.8.8). That is one test, one agent version and one provider. The larger claim is conditional: an AI far beyond human ability could be governed through a network like this only as one of several under independent control, never alone. Part 8 names what would lose the bet.
